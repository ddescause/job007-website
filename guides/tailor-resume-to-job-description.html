How to tailor your resume to every job description, with AI doing the heavy lifting

A practical method for matching your resume to a job description without lying or starting over.

Recruiters read your resume against one job, not against your career. A resume written for "any role like this" makes them do the matching work themselves, and most won't. Tailoring means doing that work for them.

It does not mean inventing experience. It means choosing, ordering and wording what you have really done so the fit is obvious within seconds.

Step 1: Read the job description like a scoring sheet

Before touching your resume, pull out of the posting:

  • The three to five must-haves: the skills or experiences mentioned first, repeated, or marked "required".
  • The words they use for those things. If they say "go-to-market", don't only say "commercial strategy".
  • The level: scope, team size, budget, who the role reports to.

Those are the lines a recruiter will look for first, and the terms an applicant tracking system will match on.

Step 2: Lead with your closest match

Rewrite your summary so its first sentence answers the must-haves directly. Then reorder the bullets under each role: the most relevant achievement goes first, even if it isn't your proudest.

Step 3: Mirror their language, honestly

Where you genuinely have the experience, describe it in the posting's vocabulary. This helps both human readers and keyword-based screening. Where you don't have it, don't claim it: interviewers check.

Step 4: Quantify what matters to this role

Numbers carry more weight than adjectives, but pick the numbers this employer cares about. Revenue for a sales role, time-to-hire for a talent role, uptime for an infrastructure role.

Step 5: Keep the format simple

Applicant tracking systems read structure. Use standard section headings (Experience, Education, Skills), a single column, real text rather than images, and a common file format. Fancy layouts often read badly when parsed.

Step 6: Write a cover letter that adds, not repeats

Use it for what the resume can't say: why this company, why now, and one story that proves your strongest match. Three short paragraphs are enough.

Where AI helps, and where it shouldn't

Tailoring every application by hand takes an hour or more, which is why most people stop doing it after the first ten. AI is good at the mechanical part: finding the must-haves, reordering, rewording in the employer's language, drafting a first cover letter.

Two rules keep it honest and in your voice:

  1. Feed it your real material. Achievements, numbers and projects you can talk about in an interview. It should select and rephrase, never invent.
  2. Edit by instruction, then check. Tell it what to change ("lead with my fintech work", "make this punchier") and read the result before it goes anywhere.
