Executive job search: a practical guide for senior leaders

How senior roles really get filled, and how to run a search that matches it.

Senior roles are filled differently from the rest of the market. Many are handled by executive search firms or through board and investor networks, and a public posting, when there is one, is often the last step rather than the first.

That changes how an executive search should run. Applying to whatever appears on a job board is the least effective thing a senior candidate can do. The work is in choosing where you belong, being known to the people who fill those roles, and showing up early with a case built for each one.

How senior roles actually get filled

Before planning your search, it helps to see the routes a VP, director or C-level role usually takes from "we need someone" to a signed offer:

  • Internal moves and succession. Many senior vacancies are filled from inside or by someone the leadership team already knows. You can't apply to these. You can only be known before they open.
  • Referrals from the board, investors and peers. When a CEO or board chair needs a leader, the first step is often asking people they trust who they would hire.
  • Retained executive search. For important hires, a company pays a search firm to map the market and bring a shortlist. The firm approaches candidates. Candidates rarely approach the firm about a specific mandate.
  • Contingent recruiters. More common for director and lower VP levels. They are paid only if their candidate is hired, so they move fast and present widely.
  • Public postings. Some senior roles are advertised, often on the company's own careers page first. Some are posted while a search firm is also working the role.

A good executive search works all of these at once. Most of them reward the same thing: being visible to the right small group of people, with a clear story, before the role exists. For more on why timing matters, see our guide to the hidden job market.

Start with a target list, not a job board

Write down 20 to 30 organizations where you would genuinely add value: by sector, stage, size and the problem they need solved. Everything else in your search hangs off this list.

Be specific about why each one is on it. "Big fintech" is not a reason. "Series C payments company expanding into Europe, no regional leader yet" is. That one line tells you who to talk to, what to say, and how to tailor your resume when a role opens.

Review the list monthly. Add companies when you hear about funding rounds, acquisitions, new CEOs or expansion, since those events often create senior roles. Drop ones where the fit turns out weaker than you thought.

Work three channels at once

  1. Your network. Former colleagues, board members, investors and advisors hear about roles before they exist. Tell a small circle, specifically, what you are looking for.
  2. Executive search firms. Build relationships with the partners who cover your function and sector. Be easy to place: a clear story, a crisp resume, realistic expectations.
  3. Company career sites. Many senior roles that are advertised appear first on the company's own careers page. Watch your target list directly rather than waiting for job boards.

Use your network with a precise ask

"Let me know if you hear of anything" gets you nothing. People help when the request is easy to act on. Name the type of role, the kind of company and one or two specific organizations, and say what you'd like them to do.

Sample note to a trusted contact

"I'm starting to think about my next role, quietly, so please keep this between us. I'm looking at COO roles in B2B software companies of 200 to 1,000 people, ideally ones that have just raised and need to scale operations. Two I'd love to know more about are [Company A] and [Company B]. Do you know anyone on their leadership team or board who'd be open to a 20-minute conversation?"

Keep this circle small while you're employed. Pick people who have a reason to help, who move in the right circles, and who you trust to keep it quiet. Follow up with a short thank-you and a one-line update a few weeks later. That keeps you in mind without asking again.

Work with executive search firms the right way

A search firm works for the hiring company, not for you. That changes how you should approach them.

  • Be easy to understand. A partner deciding whether to put you on a shortlist needs to explain you in two sentences to a client. Give them those two sentences.
  • Be honest about compensation and location. Vague answers make you harder to place. Give a realistic range and say clearly what you would and wouldn't move for.
  • Respond fast and be helpful even when the role isn't for you. Recommending someone strong for a mandate that doesn't fit you is one of the best ways to be remembered for the next one.
  • Ask them to confirm before presenting you anywhere. You want to know which companies have seen your name, both for discretion and to avoid being put forward twice by different firms.

Sharpen the story before the resume

At senior level you are hired for a problem you have solved before. Summarize your career as two or three of those, with results: a turnaround, a scale-up, an integration, a market entry. Your resume and every conversation should tell the same story.

Before and after: an executive summary

Before: "Results-driven senior executive with 20+ years of cross-functional leadership experience across multiple industries and a passion for driving growth."

After: "Operations leader who scales B2B companies through fast growth. Took a software business from one to four countries, rebuilt its supply chain after an acquisition, and led the integration of two support teams into one."

The second version tells a reader exactly what problem you solve. If you can't write it in three lines, the story isn't sharp enough yet. Test it by saying it out loud to someone outside your industry.

Keep it confidential

If you're currently in role, discretion matters more than speed. Senior moves are noticed, and a leak can damage your standing with your board and team. Use personal devices and email, keep an exclusion list of your employer, its group companies, competitors and clients, and ask firms to confirm before presenting you anywhere.

Think about references early. At this level, references are often checked through back channels as well as the names you provide, so be careful who you tell. Our guide to job searching while employed covers LinkedIn settings, scheduling and references in more detail.

Tailor for every conversation

Each opportunity deserves a version of your resume and a short note that lead with what that organization needs. It's the most time-consuming part of an executive search, and the one most people skip.

Tailoring at senior level is less about keywords and more about emphasis. For a company preparing for a sale, lead with your exit experience. For one fixing a troubled division, lead with the turnaround. The facts stay the same. The order and the space you give them change. Our guide on tailoring your resume to a job description walks through a method you can reuse.

Expect a longer process

Executive processes often run several rounds with boards, investors and peers. Keep momentum by running several conversations in parallel rather than one at a time.

A typical senior process might include a screen with the search firm, meetings with the CEO and future peers, a case or plan presentation, board or investor interviews, and detailed references. Each step can be weeks apart. If you pause your search while waiting on one company, you lose months.

A weekly rhythm that fits around a demanding job

  • Monday: check your target companies' career pages and any alerts. Note new roles and company news.
  • Midweek: one or two network conversations, by phone or over coffee, outside office hours.
  • Thursday: follow up with search firms on open mandates and send any tailored applications.
  • Weekend: one hour to update your target list, refine your story and prepare for next week's interviews.

Most executives searching while employed have only a few hours a week. Spend them on relationships and judgment, and automate the checking.

Frequently asked questions

How long does an executive job search take?

Usually longer than a search at earlier career stages. Senior processes involve more people and more rounds, and roles open less often. Plan for several months, and keep several conversations going in parallel so one slow process doesn't stall your whole search.

Should executives apply to jobs posted online?

Yes, selectively. Many senior roles are filled through networks and search firms, but some are advertised, often first on the company's own careers page. Apply to postings at companies on your target list, with a tailored resume and note, and try to find a warm route to the hiring manager at the same time.

How do I get noticed by executive search firms?

Build relationships with the partners who cover your function and sector before you need them. Make yourself easy to place with a clear two-line story and realistic expectations, respond quickly, and recommend strong people for roles that aren't right for you.

How do I keep an executive job search confidential?

Use personal devices and email, tell only a small trusted circle, keep an exclusion list of your employer, its group companies, competitors and clients, and ask search firms to confirm before presenting you to any company.

What should an executive resume focus on?

Two or three problems you have solved, with results: a turnaround, a scale-up, an integration, a market entry. Lead with the one that matters most to each organization, and keep the summary short enough to say out loud in under a minute.
