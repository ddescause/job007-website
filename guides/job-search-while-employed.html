How to job search while employed, without your employer finding out

Practical rules for a confidential search, from LinkedIn settings to interview scheduling.

Searching while you still have a job is the strongest position to negotiate from, and the most delicate one to manage. You have an income, so you can be selective. But you also have a manager, colleagues and an IT department, and none of them need to know yet.

Most confidential searches don't leak through bad luck. They leak through small, avoidable habits: a resume printed on the office printer, a LinkedIn headline rewritten on a Tuesday afternoon, an application that lands at a sister company. This guide covers the habits that matter, with examples you can copy.

Keep it off company property

Assume anything you do on company equipment or accounts can be seen. Many employers monitor email, network traffic and devices for security reasons, and keep records long after you've deleted something.

  • Use your personal laptop, phone and email. Never your work account, work device or company network, including the office Wi-Fi on your personal phone.
  • Don't store resumes, cover letters or offer letters on a company drive, and don't print them at work.
  • Check that your personal browser isn't signed in on your work laptop. Synced bookmarks and history travel both ways.
  • Keep interviews off your work calendar. A vague "personal appointment" block is enough.
  • Schedule calls from somewhere private, not a meeting room or an open-plan desk.

Use a personal email address that looks professional, ideally just your first and last name. It's the first thing a recruiter sees.

Set LinkedIn up for discretion

LinkedIn is where most leaks start, because changes there are public by default. Its settings change from time to time, so check the current wording, but the principles stay the same.

  • Use LinkedIn's "Open to Work" setting visible to recruiters only, rather than the public photo frame. LinkedIn says it tries to hide this from recruiters at your own company, but it can't guarantee it.
  • Turn off sharing of profile updates before you edit your headline or experience, so your network doesn't get a notification.
  • Update your profile gradually rather than rewriting it overnight. A refreshed summary in one week and a new skills section the next draws far less attention than a full makeover.
  • Don't follow dozens of competitors or connect with a wave of recruiters in a single day. Colleagues see activity in their feed.
  • Be careful with "likes" and comments on posts about job hunting or leaving a job. They can show up in your colleagues' feeds.

A useful test: if your manager looked at your profile today, would anything look different from last month? If yes, slow down.

Never apply to the wrong company

The fastest way to be discovered is an application landing at your own employer, a sister company, a close competitor where people know each other, or a client. Recruiters talk, and hiring managers move between these companies.

Keep a written exclusion list and check every application against it. Write it down before you start, because it's easy to forget a subsidiary when a role looks perfect.

CategoryWhat to include
Your employerThe parent company, every subsidiary and brand, and any recent acquisitions
Close partnersClients, key suppliers, agencies and consultancies that work inside your company
Tight-knit competitorsCompanies where your manager or senior colleagues used to work
Former employersOnly if you left on terms that make a call back to your current team likely

Be careful with recruiting agencies too. Ask an agency recruiter which company a role is at before you agree to let them submit your resume.

Talk to recruiters without tipping your hand

Recruiters handle employed candidates all the time, and most respect confidentiality when you ask for it clearly. Say it at the start, in writing.

Example reply to a recruiter

Thanks for reaching out. I'm currently employed and my search is confidential, so please don't share my name or resume with any company without asking me first. I'm open to a call before 9am or after 5:30pm. Could you tell me which company this role is with?

This one message sets your conditions, protects your resume, and checks the company against your exclusion list before anything goes further.

Protect your references

Tell prospective employers upfront that your current employer can't be contacted until an offer is signed. This is normal, and good employers expect it. If an offer is made "subject to references", ask for the reference check at your current employer to happen last, after everything else has cleared.

Use former managers and colleagues who have left the company as references. Contact them before you give their names, so they're ready for the call.

Example message to a former manager

I'm quietly exploring a move and I'm in final rounds for a product lead role. Would you be comfortable being a reference? The call would focus on the platform launch we did together in 2023. Please keep this between us, as I haven't told my current team.

Manage your time, not just your secrecy

  • Batch interviews early morning, at lunch or late afternoon, and use personal time off for final rounds.
  • Ask for video calls for early rounds. Travel to an on-site interview is often what makes an absence noticeable.
  • Don't dress differently on interview days. Change somewhere else if you need to.
  • Keep doing your current job well. A dip in performance draws more attention than a new headline.
  • Don't tell colleagues, however close. Once one person knows, you no longer control who else does.

When a recruiter asks for your availability, offer specific slots rather than "anytime". It looks organised and keeps interviews where you want them.

Example availability reply

I can do Tuesday or Thursday at 8:00am, or Wednesday at 12:30pm, all Eastern time. For a final round, I can take a half day if you give me a few days' notice.

A weekly routine that fits around a full-time job

A search while employed works best as a steady habit, not a burst of late nights. Here is a routine of about four hours a week:

  1. Monday morning, 30 minutes: review new roles from your saved searches and target companies. Shortlist three to five.
  2. Tuesday and Thursday evenings, 60 minutes each: tailor and send applications for the shortlist. See how to tailor your resume to each job.
  3. Wednesday lunch, 30 minutes: one networking message or coffee with someone outside your company.
  4. Weekend, 60 minutes: follow-ups, interview prep and updating your tracker.

Roles posted on company career pages often appear there before they reach the big job boards, so checking your target companies directly is worth part of your Monday slot. Our guide to the hidden job market explains where to look.

Have an answer ready for "why are you leaving?"

Every interviewer will ask, and you should never criticise your current employer. Give a short, forward-looking reason that's true: more scope, a different industry, a skill you want to build. Then move to why this role fits.

For example: "I've learned a lot leading the migration project, and I'm ready to own a product end to end. This role does that, in a market I already know."

If your employer finds out anyway

Stay calm and don't deny it if they have clear evidence. A short, professional answer usually works best: you're committed to doing your job well, you were curious about an opportunity, and you're happy to talk about your growth here. Don't resign on the spot, and don't promise to stop looking unless you mean it. Then speed up your search, because the timeline may no longer be yours.

Spend your limited hours on judgment

When you're employed, you have maybe an hour a day for your search. Spend it on decisions (which roles, which companies, what to say) rather than on searching job boards and retyping forms. Frequently asked questions

Can my employer see that I'm looking for a job?

Not unless you give them a way to. Your employer can usually see what happens on company laptops, phones, email and networks, so keep your search entirely on personal devices and accounts. Outside of that, the main risks are public signals: a LinkedIn profile that changes overnight, an application that lands at your own company or a partner, or a colleague you confided in.

Should I turn on Open to Work on LinkedIn while employed?

If you use it, choose the option that shows it to recruiters only, not the public photo frame. LinkedIn says it tries to hide this signal from recruiters at your current company, but it can't guarantee it, for example if a recruiter's company page isn't linked correctly. If even a small risk is too much, leave it off and rely on applying directly and on your network.

Can I be fired for looking for another job?

It depends on where you work and on your contract. In many places, including most US states where employment is at will, an employer can end your employment for almost any lawful reason, and that can include finding out you're leaving. Other countries give more protection. Either way, the safest plan is not to give them the information until you have a signed offer.

Should I tell my boss I'm looking for a new job?

Usually not until you have a signed offer. Once your manager knows, you may be left out of new projects, passed over for raises, or replaced on their timeline instead of yours. The exception is a manager who has openly encouraged you to grow beyond the role, and even then, it's a judgment call.

How do I schedule interviews while working full time?

Offer early morning, lunchtime or late afternoon slots for screening calls, and ask for video rather than on-site for early rounds. Save personal days or half days for final rounds. Most recruiters are used to employed candidates and will work around your hours if you give them clear options.

How long does a job search while employed usually take?

Plan for several months rather than several weeks. You have fewer hours to give it than someone searching full time, and hiring processes often run three or more rounds. A steady routine of a few hours a week tends to beat bursts of effort followed by long gaps.
